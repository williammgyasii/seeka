Sending application after application and hearing nothing is one of the most discouraging parts of looking for work. It's also extremely common, and it usually says more about how online applications work than about you.

The good news: the silence has causes, and most of them are fixable.

## Why applications disappear

### 1. The pile is enormous

Posting a job online is cheap and applying is one click. A popular opening can collect hundreds of applications. The people screening them have limited time, so they skim and filter fast.

### 2. Screening happens before a person reads your story

Applicant tracking systems and screening questions sort candidates by keywords, location, work authorisation, and years of experience. Recruiters then skim what's left. A strong candidate can be filtered out by a missing keyword or an unanswered knockout question.

### 3. You're applying to jobs that aren't a real fit

When applying feels like a numbers game, it's tempting to apply to anything close. But roles where you meet only half the requirements rarely get a reply, and they take time you could spend on roles where you're a strong match.

### 4. The job may not be open to you

Some postings can only hire in certain countries, or need a work permit you don't have. "Remote" doesn't always mean "remote from anywhere". Applications that fail these checks are usually rejected silently.

### 5. Nobody inside knows you exist

An application on its own is a cold request. A short message to the recruiter or hiring manager, or a referral from an employee, puts a person behind your name.

## Five changes that get replies

### Apply to fewer, better-fitting roles

Before you apply, check: Do I meet most of the core requirements? Can this employer hire someone where I live? Is the seniority right? If the answer to any of these is no, skip it. Ten strong applications beat a hundred weak ones.

### Mirror the job's language, honestly

Use the same words the posting uses for skills you actually have. If the job says "stakeholder management" and your résumé says "worked with other teams", you may be filtered out for a vocabulary mismatch. Never add skills you don't have — it only moves the rejection to the interview.

### Answer the screening questions carefully

Knockout questions about location, authorisation, availability, and salary decide a lot. Read them twice. If you're unsure, answer truthfully and explain briefly in a cover note.

### Contact a person for every application that matters

For each role you care about, find the recruiter or the hiring manager and send a short message: the role, one concrete reason you fit, and a small ask. ### Ask for referrals

An employee referral is often the strongest signal you can send. You don't need to know anyone yet — see [how to get a referral when you don't know anyone](/guides/how-to-get-a-referral/).

## Track what you send

Keep a simple record for each application: the date, the role, who you contacted, and when to follow up. Patterns appear quickly. If one kind of role never replies, the fit is probably off. If messages to hiring managers get answers and applications alone don't, you know where to spend your time.

## Look after yourself

Silence feels personal, but it usually isn't. Set a sustainable weekly target — for example, five well-researched applications with outreach instead of fifty cold ones — and stop when you hit it. Progress you can measure is easier to keep up than an endless queue.

## The short version

The silence usually comes from volume, filtering, poor fit, and no human contact. Apply to fewer roles that truly fit, use the job's language honestly, get the screening questions right, and put a person behind every application that matters.
