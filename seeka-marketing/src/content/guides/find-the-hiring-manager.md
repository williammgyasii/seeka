The hiring manager is the person whose team has the open seat. They decide who gets hired; the recruiter runs the process. Most job postings don't name them, but you can usually work it out in fifteen minutes.

## Hiring manager or recruiter: who should you contact?

Both, for different reasons:

- **The recruiter** controls the process: screening, scheduling, and whether your application is seen at all. They want candidates who clearly match the brief.
- **The hiring manager** feels the problem the role exists to solve. They care less about keywords and more about whether you can do the work.

If you can only reach one, reach the person closest to the work for specialist roles (engineering, design, finance) and the recruiter for high-volume roles (support, sales development, retail management).

## Step 1: Read the posting for clues

The job description usually tells you more than it seems:

- **"Reports to"** lines name the title, sometimes the person.
- **The team name** ("Payments Platform", "EMEA Customer Success") narrows the search.
- **The level of the role** tells you the manager's level: a senior individual contributor usually reports to a manager or director; a manager usually reports to a director or head of department.
- **The location** of the team helps when a company has offices in several countries.

Write down: team name, likely manager title, and location.

## Step 2: Search LinkedIn the right way

Search for people at the company with the likely title and team. For example: `"Head of Customer Success" Northwind` or `Engineering Manager Payments Northwind`.

Then check:

- **Who recently shared the job.** Hiring managers often post their own openings with "My team is hiring".
- **Who has the matching team in their headline** ("Leading the Payments Platform team").
- **Who joined or was promoted recently.** New managers often build teams.

If the company is large and you find several candidates, the one in the same location and the right seniority is usually your person.

## Step 3: Check the company's own pages

- **The team or leadership page** lists heads of department at smaller companies.
- **The engineering or product blog** names the people who write about the team's work.
- **Conference talks and podcasts** often feature team leads describing what they're building, which also gives you something specific to mention.

## Step 4: Find a way to reach them

In order of preference:

1. **A warm introduction** from someone who knows them. Check shared connections first.
2. **A LinkedIn message.** If you're not connected, send a connection request with a short note (under 300 characters).
3. **Email.** Many companies use a predictable pattern such as `firstname@company.com` or `firstname.lastname@company.com`. If you guess an address, say so honestly and keep the message brief. Don't buy scraped lists or spam several guesses.

## Step 5: Say something worth replying to

Hiring managers are busy and wary of mass outreach. Be specific about the role and about one thing you've done that matches their problem:

> Hi Dana — I saw you're hiring a Senior HR Business Partner for the operations org. I led onboarding redesign for a 300-person team at Kilo and cut time-to-productivity from eight weeks to five. I've applied through the site and would value five minutes to hear what matters most to you in this hire.

Mention that you've already applied. It shows respect for their process and gives them an easy next step: look you up.

## What not to do

- **Don't message everyone in the department.** One or two well-chosen people is enough.
- **Don't ask them to "check out my profile".** Tell them the one thing that matters.
- **Don't send your full life story.** Keep the first message to four or five sentences.
- **Don't go around the process.** Contacting the hiring manager complements your application; it doesn't replace it.

## The short version

Use the posting to work out the team and the manager's likely title. Find them on LinkedIn or the company's own pages. Reach them warmly if you can, directly if you can't, and say one specific thing about their problem and your experience.
